# Final Fantasy Mystic Quest

*Final Fantasy Mystic Quest* is a 1992 Super NES spin-off designed from the start as a beginner-friendly RPG aimed at the North American market. Square marketed it as a simplified entry point for players who might find the mainline games too complex or long, with visible enemies on the field instead of random battles, automatic equipment upgrades, a two-character party where the partner can act on its own, and a more linear path through the world. Weapons also double as tools for exploration (axes for chopping trees, bombs for clearing obstacles, claws as grappling hooks).

It was the first *Final Fantasy* game released in Europe.

### Naming

The title changes by region, which still causes occasional confusion:

- North America: *Final Fantasy Mystic Quest* (sometimes styled *Final Fantasy: Mystic Quest*)
- Japan: *Final Fantasy USA: Mystic Quest* (released almost a year later)
- Europe / PAL: *Mystic Quest Legend*

The European rename was deliberate. *Final Fantasy Adventure* (the first *Mana* / *Seiken Densetsu* game) had already come out in Europe under the title *Mystic Quest*, so Square and Nintendo avoided the collision by adding "Legend." Only the North American version shows the words "FINAL FANTASY" on the title screen; the Japanese and European versions do not.

### Release history

- Super NES / Super Famicom: North America October 5, 1992; Japan September 10, 1993; Europe October 1993
- Wii Virtual Console: Europe September 24, 2010; North America October 18, 2010; Japan December 21, 2010
- Wii U Virtual Console: Japan April 16, 2014

It was sold at a lower price point than other Square RPGs of the time (around $40 in the US) and came with a strategy guide in some regions to help new players.

### Reception

Contemporary reviews were mixed to lukewarm. Critics and players noted the short length, simplified systems, and thin story. Longtime *Final Fantasy* fans often felt talked down to, while the casual audience Square hoped to attract largely ignored it the same way they ignored other JRPGs. Square later reported roughly 800,000 units sold worldwide, about half of those in Japan.

Over time it settled into a reputation as the black sheep or "baby's first Final Fantasy." Defenders still point to the solid soundtrack (especially the battle and boss themes), the oversized enemy sprites, the lack of random encounters, and the fact that it actually does what it set out to do: give a complete, low-friction RPG experience to someone who has never played one. It has never received a major remake or remaster beyond the Virtual Console ports.
